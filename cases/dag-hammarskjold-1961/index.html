Unsolved September 18, 1961 Suspicious Death

Dag Hammarskjöld

UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld, 56, and 15 others died when their DC-6 crashed near Ndola, Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia), shortly after midnight on September 18, 1961, as he travelled to ceasefire talks in the Congo crisis. Early inquiries pointed to pilot error or left the cause open. A UN-mandated inquiry reported in 2024 that an external attack or threat remains plausible; the cause is undetermined.

Case background and investigation

Dag Hammarskjöld, a Swedish diplomat, had served as Secretary-General of the United Nations since April 1953. In September 1961 he was travelling to Ndola, in what was then Northern Rhodesia, to negotiate a ceasefire between UN peacekeepers and separatist forces in the Congolese province of Katanga, according to UN News. He was flying in a chartered Douglas DC-6 registered SE-BDY, which Wikipedia's article on the crash identifies as operated by Transair Sweden.

The aircraft crashed shortly after midnight on the night of September 17-18, 1961, near Ndola. Sixteen people were on board. UN News reports that all but one died at the scene and that the single survivor died of his injuries some days later. Wikipedia names the survivor as Sergeant Harold Julien, a UN security officer, and says he died in hospital five days after the crash; it places the wreckage about 15 kilometres from Ndola airport.

According to Wikipedia, a Rhodesian Board of Investigation worked from September to November 1961 and found no evidence of a bomb or missile, concluding that the aircraft had been flown too low and struck trees. A Rhodesian Commission of Inquiry followed in January 1962, and a UN Commission of Investigation reported later in 1962 without being able to determine the cause. UN News notes that the initial Rhodesian finding of pilot error was disputed. It also summarises eyewitness accounts suggesting that more than one aircraft was in the air, that SE-BDY was on fire before it came down, or that it was fired upon.

Interest revived in the 2010s. Wikipedia reports that an unofficial commission of jurists chaired by Stephen Sedley recommended in September 2013 that the UN reopen its inquiry, and that Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon appointed an independent panel in March 2015, headed by Mohamed Chande Othman, a former Chief Justice of Tanzania. Othman has since led successive UN inquiries as 'Eminent Person'. An AFP report from December 2019 states that his report that year criticised the United States and Britain for withholding information, and that the General Assembly extended the investigation by consensus.

The most recent mandate was given in December 2022, according to UN News. When Othman's report was transmitted to the General Assembly in October 2024, a UN spokesperson said Othman considered it still plausible that an external attack or threat was a cause of the crash, with sabotage or unintentional human error as the remaining alternative explanations. New information cited included probable intercepts of communications, the capacity of Katangan or other forces to attack the aircraft, and the presence of foreign paramilitary or intelligence personnel in the area.

Othman also assessed it as almost certain that relevant undisclosed material remains in the archives of some member states. No person or state has been found responsible, and the cause of the crash remains officially undetermined.
