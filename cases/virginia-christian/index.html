Virginia Christian Case

Ida Belote, 51, was killed in her Hampton, Virginia, home on March 18, 1912. Her 16-year-old laundress, Virginia Christian, was convicted of first-degree murder by an all-white, all-male jury in April 1912 and was electrocuted at the Virginia State Penitentiary on August 16, 1912, a day after turning 17. She was the first woman put to death in Virginia's electric chair.

Case background and investigation

Ida Virginia Belote was a widow living in Hampton, Virginia. According to the Library of Virginia, she was born in North Carolina in March 1861, married James E. W. Belote in 1879, and had eight children; her husband died in June 1911. Virginia Christian, a Black teenager born in Hampton on August 15, 1895, worked for Belote as a laundress. Accounts of the case say Christian had left school at about 13 to help support her family after her mother became paralyzed.

On March 18, 1912, Belote was killed in her home. According to accounts of the trial, Belote had accused Christian of stealing a skirt and a locket, and a confrontation followed in which Belote was struck with a broom handle and a towel was forced into her throat, causing her to suffocate. The Library of Virginia notes that two of Belote's young daughters found their mother's body and later gave testimony at the coroner's inquisition. Christian was arrested the same day.

Christian was indicted within days and tried in Elizabeth City County on April 8 and 9, 1912. The jury was made up entirely of white men. Her attorneys, who included J. Thomas Newsome, argued that the killing had not been planned. The jury deliberated for 23 minutes before finding her guilty of first-degree murder, and she was sentenced to death. Virginia's highest court declined to review the case in June 1912.

Christian's family, her lawyers and others petitioned Governor William Hodges Mann to commute the sentence, citing her age. Records held by the Library of Virginia include letters from both the Christian and Belote families as well as from the prosecutor. The governor granted a short reprieve but refused to commute the sentence.

Christian was electrocuted at the Virginia State Penitentiary in Richmond on the morning of August 16, 1912, one day after her seventeenth birthday. According to the Library of Virginia, she remained the only woman executed by the Commonwealth after executions were centralized at the penitentiary in 1908 until Teresa Lewis was put to death in 2010. She is also described as the last female juvenile offender executed in the United States.

The case has continued to draw attention from historians because of the defendant's age, the composition of the jury and the speed of the proceedings. In 2025 the Hampton History Museum hosted a public program re-examining the case.
