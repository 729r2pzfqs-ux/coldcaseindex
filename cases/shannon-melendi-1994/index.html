Shannon Melendi

Shannon Melendi, a 19-year-old Emory University student, was abducted from a DeKalb County softball complex in 1994 and never seen again. Umpire Colvin "Butch" Hinton was convicted of her murder in 2005 and later confessed, though her body was never recovered.

Case Background & Investigation

Shannon Melendi was a 19-year-old sophomore at Emory University in Atlanta, Georgia, and a native of Miami, Florida, known as a bright, ambitious student. To earn extra money she took a part-time job keeping score at the Softball Country Club, a recreation complex in DeKalb County. On Saturday, March 26, 1994, she left the field during a break, telling coworkers she would return. She was never seen again, vanishing in what became one of metro Atlanta's most haunting abduction cases.

For years the case stalled with no body, no crime scene, and no clear suspect. Suspicion eventually settled on Colvin "Butch" Hinton, an airline mechanic who moonlighted as an umpire at the same softball complex and who had a prior conviction for abducting and sexually assaulting a teenage girl. Investigators came to believe Hinton had lured Shannon away under a pretext, then kidnapped, raped, and murdered her, disposing of her remains so thoroughly that they were never found.

In 2004, a decade after Shannon disappeared, Hinton was indicted for her murder. At trial in 2005, prosecutors built their case without a body or a physical crime scene, relying on Hinton's history, witness testimony, and circumstantial evidence. He was convicted and sentenced to life in prison, a landmark result described as the first time in Georgia history that a defendant was convicted of murder without a body or an identified crime scene.

While incarcerated, Hinton confessed in detail to the killing. He told authorities he had abducted Shannon at knifepoint from the softball field, taken her to his home, and murdered her, then burned her remains and scattered the ashes, ensuring they could never be recovered. He expressed remorse, saying he hated what he had done and knew he would never be forgiven, though for Shannon's family his words offered no place to bury their daughter.

Under Georgia's sentencing rules at the time, Hinton became eligible for parole roughly every seven years, and each hearing has reopened old wounds for the Melendi family. They have repeatedly petitioned and gathered signatures to keep him imprisoned, and the parole board has denied his release each time it has been considered. Shannon's mother died without ever recovering her daughter's remains. More than three decades after Shannon vanished, her family continues to advocate against Hinton's release and to promote personal-safety education in her memory, keeping alive the story of a young woman whose life was cut short and whose body was never found.
